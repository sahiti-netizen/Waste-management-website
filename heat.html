Heat is not gender-neutral.

The people who clear India's waste, sell on its footpaths and stitch in its one-room homes are overwhelmingly women — and they work in the hottest, least protected conditions in the country. India currently has no enforceable temperature at which any worker acquires a right to stop.

>70% of India's informal waste sector workers are women — about 90% of street-level recyclers in Pune
~80% of surveyed SEWA members reported heat-related income loss (up to 50%), health harm, or both
89% of 519 Delhi street vendors had no access to free, clean water at work; over 70% had no toilet
247bn potential labour-hours India lost to heat in 2024 — up 124% on the 1990s (Lancet Countdown)
The mechanisms

Four separate channels, four different fixes.

"Women are more vulnerable to heat" is true and analytically useless. The exposure arrives through four independent routes — and each one needs a different intervention.

1 · Physiology

Women sweat roughly 15% less than men under identical conditions, retaining more heat for the same exposure. Heat stress also disrupts menstrual cycles and intensifies menopausal symptoms — and high anaemia prevalence cuts oxygen delivery exactly when thermal load raises cardiovascular demand.

→ Exposure limits can't be set from male-referenced thresholds.

2 · Paid by output, not by hour

Most of this work pays per kilo, per brick, per plate sold. Resting in the heat isn't a safety adjustment — it's a pay cut. A MGNREGS worker whose site crosses 38°C by 10am is docked if she misses her earthwork target and unpaid if she leaves. The advisory and the wage rule contradict each other; the wage rule wins.

→ Protection has to attach to income, not just behaviour.

3 · The sanitation–hydration trap

Rehydrating means urinating; urinating means a toilet; there is no toilet. So women drink less. Among 200 women with inadequate workplace toilets, 87% reported recurring genitourinary problems; those without toilet access carried six times the infection risk, and those drinking too little four times.

→ Free water and safe women's toilets are heat infrastructure.

4 · The second shift

Indian women spend 46% of waking hours on unpaid care — eight times men. Heatwaves add about 90 minutes a day to it. For home-based workers the house is the worksite, so exposure never ends: indoor productivity falls ~2% per °C above 25°C, and slum settlements can run 6°C hotter than the neighbourhoods around them.

→ The dwelling and the night are workplace exposure.

Heat cuts informal women's income from four directions at once — fewer working hours, fewer customers, spoiled stock, and new costs for water, ice and medicine. A salaried worker in an air-conditioned office loses none of the four.

And nobody is counting properly

For 2024, the health ministry recorded 360 heatstroke deaths; a media-verified count across 17 states found 733 deaths and over 40,000 heatstroke cases. For 2025, the NCDC logged 7,192 suspected heatstroke cases and 14 confirmed deaths.

India's heat deaths are disaggregated neither by sex nor by occupation. A European estimate for summer 2022 found roughly 56% more heat deaths in women than men, and a 30-year analysis of Indian mortality data finds women's heat deaths rising since 2005 while men's fell — but sex-specific attributable mortality for India remains genuinely unresolved. That absence is itself the finding: you cannot target what you do not count.

The sharpest case

Waste pickers work the hottest surface in the city.

Between 1.5 and 4 million people work India's informal waste sector. Over 70% are women. Together they handle 60–70% of urban recyclable waste and underpin roughly 70% of the country's plastic recycling — an unpaid climate mitigation subsidy from the poorest women in the city to everyone else in it.

Landfill surfaces reach up to 70°C. Delhi's ambient peaks hit 49°C. Every summer Ghazipur, Bhalswa and Okhla catch fire as decomposing waste generates methane that ignites in the heat — satellites have picked up 124 significant methane leaks over Delhi since 2020, including a Ghazipur plume venting 156 tonnes an hour. Women work through the smoke, because the scrap price doesn't fall when the temperature rises.

These women are simultaneously the most heat-exposed workers in the city, essential municipal infrastructure operating without a contract, and net contributors to climate mitigation. If a heat regime can't reach them, it isn't a heat regime.

What already works

SEWA's parametric heat insurance pays out automatically when temperature crosses a threshold — no claim, no assessor. It scaled from 21,000 women in 2023 to about 250,000 across 34 districts in 2025.

…and its honest limit

The 2024 season paid ₹2.92 crore to 46,242 women — an average near US$7 each. That's ORS and medicine, not income replacement. What it proves is the rail: objective trigger, zero claims friction, money to women, at scale.

Cool roofs

Mahila Housing Trust's reflective paint and insulated roofing cut indoor temperatures 4–7°C in Ahmedabad slum homes. One Bhopal tailor's daily income went from ₹150 to ₹450 once she could work through the afternoon.

Cooperatives

SWaCH Pune — 3,500+ member-owners, mostly women and Dalits — serves 600,000 homes and recycles 50,000+ tonnes a year. Hasiru Dala has organised 9,500 waste pickers and won occupational ID cards, the precondition for every other entitlement.

The gap

Policy sees "heat". Policy sees "women". It almost never sees both.

No heat standard in labour law

The 2020 consolidation into four labour codes produced no temperature or ventilation thresholds, even for factories. The OSH Code mentions temperature only for dock workers, doesn't recognise heat as an occupational hazard, and excludes gig workers outright.

Heat Action Plans treat women as one undifferentiated group

A Centre for Policy Research assessment of 37 plans across 18 states found gender handled as a homogeneous category rather than intersecting with income, caste and livelihood. Vulnerable-occupation lists name vendors, mechanics and construction workers — and omit domestic, home-based and gig work, which is where women concentrate. Not one plan that addressed occupation also acknowledged caste, despite waste picking and salt-pan work being caste-assigned occupations.

Heat is still not a national disaster

At least ten states have notified heatwaves as state-specific disasters. Heatwave remains outside the Disaster Management Act, 2005 — which keeps the national relief pool closed and leaves interstate migrant workers, who fall between state schemes, with no route at all.

Registration is not protection

e-Shram holds 31.89 crore unorganised workers, 54% of them women. There is no heat trigger attached to any of it.

What to do

Four things that need no new law. Then the law.

  1. Free water and safe toilets at work, mandated and audited. 100% coverage of free drinking water and shaded rest points within 200m of every registered vending zone, market, transfer station and dumpsite — with free, women-designated, lockable toilets and quarterly public reporting. Highest-return item on this list.
  2. Heat-adjust public employment. Relax MGNREGS output targets without docking wages during declared heat alerts; shift permissible hours earlier; protect ASHA and Anganwadi incentive payments when field visits are unsafe.
  3. Count properly. Sex- and occupation-disaggregated recording of heat morbidity and mortality, with one case definition shared across NCDC, NCRB and state health departments.
  4. Fund cool roofs for home-based workers. A home-based worker's roof is workplace infrastructure. Subsidise it as such.
  5. Legislate a binding occupational heat standard. Enforceable WBGT thresholds with work–rest ratios, shade and water — covering gig, platform, home-based and self-employed workers, with limits that account for pregnancy and for women's thermoregulatory baseline.
  6. Notify heatwave as a national disaster under the Disaster Management Act, 2005, so relief reaches interstate migrants too.
  7. Build state wage compensation on the parametric rail. The trigger-and-transfer plumbing is proven at 250,000 women. Put a state-sized transfer through it.
  8. Rewrite Heat Action Plans with occupation × gender × caste as the unit of vulnerability, co-designed with worker organisations rather than consulted after drafting — and add heat audits of informal workplaces employing women.
  9. Treat night heat and indoor heat as policy objects: overnight cooling centres, retrofit subsidies, and actual measurement of indoor temperature where home-based work happens.
  10. Formalise waste work. Occupational ID cards, cooperative contracts with urban local bodies, payment for the recycling and avoided emissions already being delivered — with heat provisions written into the contract.
Hyderabad

Telangana is ahead on paper. The gap is verification.

Telangana has notified heatwave as a state-specific disaster, published a 2025 Heatwave Action Plan, raised heat-death ex gratia from ₹50,000 to ₹4 lakh, and directed its labour welfare department to provide rest, drinking water, ORS and shaded rest areas specifically to sanitation and solid waste management workers. That is better than most Indian cities have.

GHMC's waste system runs on roughly 22,000 staff and about 2,000 Swachh Autos, alongside informal pickers at transfer stations and the dumpsite, with self-help-group structures that make the collection end heavily female. The Telangana study of 394 municipal solid waste workers found musculoskeletal morbidity in 76.6% and injuries in 39.7% of the Hyderabad sample — before heat enters the picture.

Where are the shaded rest points, water points and functioning women's toilets on the collection routes, at the transfer stations and at the dumpsite — and how many of them are actually open during a heat alert?

That is a mappable, auditable, ward-level dataset. A platform that already maps garbage by ward is the natural instrument for mapping the amenities the people who clear that garbage are legally owed.

Figures here come from three tiers: peer-reviewed studies (strongest, but small and geographically specific); institutional datasets (ILO, Lancet Countdown, PLFS, e-Shram, CPR); and organisational surveys reported through media (SEWA, WIEGO, MSSRF), which are directionally consistent across independent sources but whose exact coefficients should be quoted with attribution rather than as settled fact.
