# Heat Is Not Gender-Neutral

### How heatwaves hit women in India's informal economy — evidence, mechanisms, and the policy gap

*Research brief · Last updated August 2026*

---

## 0. The short version

1. **Women are the majority of India's informally employed, and a majority of the workers most exposed to heat.** Over 80% of India's women workers are in informal employment. Women are 54% of the 31.9 crore workers registered on e-Shram. They are over 70% of the informal waste sector, ~90% of street-level recyclers in Pune, and the bulk of home-based work.
2. **Heat reaches them through four distinct channels, not one.** Physiology (women sweat ~15% less than men under identical conditions), occupational exposure (unshaded, unregulated, paid by output), the sanitation–hydration trap (no toilets → drink less → dehydration and urogenital disease), and the second shift (unpaid care, cooking heat, indoor heat, hot nights).
3. **The income shock is large and immediate.** In a SEWA survey of ~600 members, nearly 80% reported heat-related loss of income (up to 50%), health impacts, or both. Delhi-NCR street-vendor surveys found 96% reporting a collapse in footfall and 90% cutting hours. A 2025 MSSRF study of 3,300+ women across seven states found 70% reporting heat-related physical symptoms and 97% reporting no institutional support of any kind.
4. **The health effects are chronic, reproductive, and undercounted.** 87% of women with inadequate workplace toilets report recurrent genitourinary problems; occupational heat exposure in a Chennai cohort of 800 pregnant women was associated with elevated risk of miscarriage and stillbirth. India's official heatstroke death counts (14 confirmed deaths in 2025) are irreconcilable with media-verified tolls (733 in 2024) — and none of them are disaggregated by sex *and* occupation.
5. **Heat also arrives as violence.** Across ~195,000 partnered women in India, Nepal and Pakistan, each 1°C of annual mean warming was associated with a 4.5% rise in intimate partner violence — 8% for physical violence in India, the highest of the three.
6. **Policy sees "heat" and it sees "women", but almost never both at once.** India's labour codes contain no enforceable heat standard. Heat Action Plans treat women as a homogeneous vulnerable group; not one assessed plan combined occupation with caste. Heatwaves are still not a nationally notified disaster.
7. **What works is already running at small scale** — SEWA's parametric heat insurance (21,000 women in 2023 → ~250,000 in 2025), Mahila Housing Trust's cool roofs (4–7°C indoors), waste-picker cooperatives like SWaCH and Hasiru Dala. None of it is at the scale of the exposure.

## 1. Who we are talking about

India's informal economy is not a residual category. It is the labour market.

| Indicator | Figure | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Share of India's workforce exposed to heat-related stress | ~75% (~380 million people) | World Bank / McKinsey analysis |
| Women's employment in India's informal sector | 81.1% | ILO |
| Female labour force participation (PLFS 2023-24) | 41.7%, up from 37% in 2022-23 | MoSPI |
| Share of all workers self-employed (PLFS 2023-24) | 58.4% | MoSPI |
| Unorganised workers registered on e-Shram | 31.89 crore | Ministry of Labour & Employment |
| Women's share of e-Shram registrations | 54.28% (≈17.31 crore) | Ministry of Labour & Employment |
| People working in India's informal waste sector | 1.5–4 million | Multiple estimates |
| Women's share of the informal waste sector | >70% (≈90% of street-level pickers in Pune) | Ocean Conservancy; sector surveys |

Two facts about that table matter more than the individual numbers.

**First: the recent growth in women's work is growth in *exposed* work.** The near-5-point jump in female labour force participation in 2023-24 was driven largely by self-employment and unpaid family labour — categories with no employer, no worksite standard, no paid leave, and no wage floor. More women in the labour force, under current rules, means more women with no protection when the temperature crosses 45°C.

**Second: registration is not protection.** e-Shram has 31.89 crore names on it, a majority of them women's. It was built as a social registry to route welfare. Benefit *utilisation* remains the gap — a database of the vulnerable is not a transfer to the vulnerable, and no heat-triggered benefit is attached to it at national scale.

## 2. Why heat is a gendered hazard: four compounding mechanisms

The common framing — "women are more vulnerable to heat" — is true but analytically useless, because it collapses four independent mechanisms that require four different interventions.

### 2.1 Physiology: the body starts from behind

Women sweat approximately **15% less than men** under identical thermal conditions, retaining more heat for the same exposure. Thermoregulation is further modulated by body composition, metabolic rate, and hormonal cycling. Heat stress can disrupt the endocrine system, producing irregular menstrual cycles, and intensify menopausal symptoms — hot flashes, insomnia, mood disturbance — creating a compounding loop for women in their 40s and 50s, who are heavily represented in waste picking, domestic work and agricultural labour.

Anaemia — with a prevalence among Indian women of reproductive age that has stayed stubbornly high — reduces oxygen-carrying capacity precisely when cardiovascular demand from thermal load rises. The physiological baseline is not neutral before the first hour of work.

**Intervention implied:** heat exposure limits and rest–work ratios cannot be set from male-referenced thresholds.

### 2.2 Occupational exposure: unshaded, unmetered, paid by output

The single most damaging structural feature is that most of this work is **paid by piece or by sale, not by hour**. A worker paid by the kilo of recyclables, the brick moulded, the cubic metre of earth moved, or the plate sold has no economic mechanism for resting. Slowing down in the heat is not a safety adjustment; it is a pay cut.

This is why heat advisories that say "avoid work between 12 and 4 pm" fail at the point of contact. For a MGNREGS worker in Jhunjhunu whose worksite crosses 38°C by 10 am, failing to meet the daily earthwork target means the wage is docked; leaving the site means no pay at all. The advisory and the wage rule contradict each other, and the wage rule is the one that is enforced.

**Intervention implied:** heat protection must be attached to *income* (guaranteed wage during declared heat days, output-target relaxation), not only to *behaviour*.

### 2.3 The sanitation–hydration trap

This mechanism is specific to women and is the clearest evidence that heat policy written for a generic worker will not protect them.

Rehydration in extreme heat requires drinking frequently. Drinking frequently requires urinating frequently. Urinating frequently requires a toilet. In a WIEGO survey of **519 street vendors across 17 Delhi markets** ahead of summer 2025, **89% had no access to free, clean water while working** and **more than 70% had no toilet access at all** — and where toilets existed, most charged, and very few were designated for women.

The documented behavioural response is to **drink less**. The measured consequences, from a study of 200 women workers with inadequate or absent workplace toilets:

- **87%** reported periodic genitourinary problems.
- Women without toilet access had a **six-fold** higher risk of urinary and genital infection.
- Women not drinking sufficient water had a **four-fold** higher risk of genitourinary problems.
- **12%** showed clinical signs of dehydration; **56%** reported symptoms consistent with it.

The downstream risks are urinary tract infection, kidney stones, and acute kidney injury. Some women also reduce food intake for the same reason, adding a nutritional insult to the thermal one.

Vendors in Delhi-NCR reported spending **₹50–300 a day** buying or carrying water — up to **₹6,000 a month** in some cases — out of earnings that are unstable to begin with. Heat converts a public infrastructure failure into a private, gendered, recurring expense.

**Intervention implied:** free water and safe, free, women-designated toilets within walking distance of vending and work zones is a *heat* intervention, not merely a sanitation one.

### 2.4 The second shift: unpaid care, indoor heat, and nights that do not cool

Paid exposure ends; thermal exposure does not.

- Indian women spend **46% of their waking hours on unpaid care work — roughly eight times what men spend.**
- Heatwaves add an estimated **90 minutes of care work per day** for women in India: more water to fetch, more people ill, more children home from closed schools.
- In the SEWA survey, **~75%** reported adverse effects on children who could not attend school during extreme temperatures. School closure is a heat policy that transfers the cost onto women's time.

For **home-based workers** — stitchers, rollers, assemblers, packers — the home *is* the worksite, so there is no "after work" at all. Indoor productivity falls roughly **2% for every 1°C above 25°C**. A 2022 HomeNet South Asia study found **43% of home-based workers reporting income decline from climate stress**. Materials make it worse: adhesives, rubber and synthetics used in gilet jewellery in Mathura, sports goods in Jalandhar, and plastic packing in Ahmedabad both trap heat and release fumes into unventilated rooms. In trades where women's daily earnings can hover near **₹24**, a few lost hours is not a marginal loss.

The built environment closes the trap. Mumbai's slum settlements run about **6°C hotter** than neighbouring areas. Tin, asbestos and thin concrete roofs store daytime heat and release it at night; urban heat islands keep night-time minimums elevated; and reliable fans, coolers or power are not a given. Sleep loss compounds into impaired concentration, weakened immunity and lower next-day productivity — a slow-acting economic loss no heat-death statistic captures.

**Intervention implied:** heat policy must treat the dwelling and the night as workplace exposure, because for millions of women they are.

## 3. What the heat actually does: evidence by outcome

### 3.1 Income and work

**Macro.** The Lancet Countdown's 2025 India data sheet puts 2024 losses at **247 billion potential labour-hours (419 hours per person)** — a **124% increase over 1990–99** — concentrated in agriculture (66%) and construction (20%), with potential income loss around **US$194 billion**. The ILO's *Working on a Warmer Planet* projects India losing **5.8% of working hours by 2030**, equivalent to **34 million full-time jobs**, up from 4.3% in 1995. World Bank/McKinsey analysis puts **up to 4.5% of India's GDP at risk by 2030** from lost labour hours.

**Micro, and gendered.** The macro numbers hide the distribution. What the field surveys show:

| Finding | Population | Source |
|---|---|---|
| ~80% reported heat-related income loss (up to 50%), health impacts, or both | ~600 SEWA members: farmers, salt-pan workers, street vendors, waste recyclers, head-loaders, construction workers | SEWA / Arsht-Rock research |
| 96% reported significant decline in customer footfall; 90% reduced working hours; >70% material losses | Street vendors, Delhi-NCR | WIEGO |
| 70% reported heat-related physical symptoms; 97% reported no institutional support to cope | 3,300+ women, 15 districts, 7 states | MSSRF, 2025 |
| 43% reported income decline from climate stress | Home-based workers, South Asia | HomeNet South Asia, 2022 |
| Productivity loss of 5.55–6.48% per 1°C WBGT rise; linear decline above 34.9°C | Female brick moulders, West Bengal | *Global Health Action*, 2014 |
| ~2% productivity loss per 1°C above 25°C indoors | Home-based workers | Cited in IndiaSpend reporting |

Reported elasticities of informal earnings to temperature — on the order of a **13% fall in gross and 19% in net earnings per 1°C**, rising toward **40% during a heatwave** — circulate widely in Indian reporting. Treat the exact coefficients with caution (they come from small, sector-specific samples and are usually relayed second-hand), but the direction and order of magnitude are consistent across every independent survey above.

**The mechanism is asymmetric.** Heat cuts income from both ends simultaneously: fewer productive hours *and* fewer customers *and* spoiled stock (vegetables, cooked food, flowers) *and* new costs (water, ice, medicines, electricity). A salaried worker in an air-conditioned office loses none of these. A woman selling vegetables loses all four at once.

### 3.2 Acute and chronic health

Documented across gig, waste, construction and vending work: heat exhaustion, heatstroke, dehydration, heat oedema, skin disease and rashes, gastrointestinal illness, and kidney-related illness. A study by the Telangana Gig and Platform Workers Union with HeatWatch documented this cluster among workers doing 12-hour days with little or no shade, water or toilets.

For waste workers specifically, thermal stress layers onto an already severe occupational profile. A cross-sectional study of **394 municipal solid waste workers in Telangana** (194 in Hyderabad, 152 in Karimnagar) found musculoskeletal problems in **76.6%** and injuries reported by **39.7%** of the Hyderabad workers. A 2026 cross-sectional survey of women waste pickers in Tamil Nadu found that greater contact with hazardous waste predicted higher health vulnerability — and, importantly, that **access to welfare support predicted lower severity**. The institutional variable is not decorative.

### 3.3 Reproductive and maternal health

This is where the evidence has firmed up fastest and where policy is furthest behind.

- A **prospective cohort of 800 pregnant women in Chennai** (published in *BJOG*, 2024) found high occupational heat exposure positively associated with **miscarriage and stillbirth**. "Heat exposed" was defined against the threshold limit values for safe manual work — max WBGT of **27.5°C for heavy** and **28.0°C for moderate** workloads. Core body temperature and urine specific gravity were measured before and after shifts.
- A **panel analysis of Bihar, 2009–2019** found temperature extremes associated with stillbirth risk.
- A systematic review of low- and middle-income countries found three of four stillbirth studies showing significant heat associations.
- The **HiP-India** consortium is now running a prospective cohort to identify the physiological mechanisms and the critical exposure windows in pregnancy.

The occupational threshold is the operative number. **27.5°C WBGT** is not an exotic condition in an Indian summer; it is an ordinary morning. Yet no Indian labour instrument requires an employer, contractor or platform to measure it, let alone act on it.

Menstrual health is the parallel, less-studied burden. In the salt pans of the Little Rann of Kutch — where up to **50,000 seasonal workers** live for eight months without electricity, healthcare or permanent shelter, temperatures routinely exceed 45°C and reach 47–48°C, and a water tanker may arrive **once every 25 days** — the absence of clean water, soap, pads and toilets produces documented infection and menstrual hygiene crises. Women brick workers report the same pattern: moulding and carrying in unshaded yards with no menstrual hygiene support.

### 3.4 Mental health, and violence

**Mental health.** Nearly **40%** of surveyed SEWA members reported worsened mental health. Field research with outdoor women workers documents anxiety, emotional fatigue and sleeplessness. Debt is the transmission mechanism: heat cuts income and raises expenditure in the same week, and the gap is closed with borrowing.

**Violence.** Across **~195,000 ever-partnered women** in India, Nepal and Pakistan, each **1°C** rise in annual mean temperature was associated with a **4.5% increase in intimate partner violence**. India had both the highest baseline prevalence and the sharpest response: **+8% physical violence** and **+7.3% sexual violence per 1°C**. Under an unlimited-emissions scenario, the study projects India's IPV prevalence rising **23.5% by the 2090s**. Domestic violence *indoors* rises most sharply during hot periods. SEWA's own grassroots leaders independently flagged that reduced income during heatwaves has worsened domestic violence.

This is the finding most often left out of heat briefings, and it is the one that most clearly makes heat a women's-safety issue rather than only an occupational-health one.

### 3.5 Mortality — and the counting problem

India cannot currently answer the question "how many women died of heat last year, and what work were they doing?"

| Source | Period | Figure |
|---|---|---|
| Ministry of Health & Family Welfare | 2024 | 360 heatstroke deaths |
| HeatWatch (media-verified, 17 states) | Mar–Jun 2024 | 733 deaths; 40,000+ heatstroke cases |
| NCDC | 1 Mar – 24 Jun 2025 | 7,192 suspected heatstroke cases; **14 confirmed deaths** |
| HeatWatch (media-verified) | Feb–Jul 2025 | ≥84 suspected deaths |

Three government sources — NCRB, NCDC and IMD — tell materially different stories, attributed to inconsistent case definitions, low clinical awareness of heat attribution, and weak implementation of reporting guidelines.

On sex disaggregation, the honest statement is this: a European estimate for 30 May–4 September 2022 found roughly **56% more heat-related deaths in women than men**, and a 30-year analysis of Indian gender-stratified mortality data (University of Michigan with Cambridge collaborators) finds heat deaths among Indian women rising since 2005 while men's fell. But sex-specific *attributable* mortality for India remains genuinely unresolved — which the researchers themselves name as the central obstacle to designing heatwave interventions for the Global South. Anyone quoting a single confident Indian figure for the gender gap in heat deaths is over-reading the evidence.

**The absence is the finding.** You cannot target what you do not count, and India counts heat deaths neither by sex nor by occupation.

## 4. Sector profiles

### Waste pickers — the most heat-exposed women in urban India

Between **1.5 and 4 million** people work India's informal waste sector; **over 70% are women**, rising to about **90%** of street-level recyclers in Pune. They handle **60–70% of urban recyclable waste** and underpin roughly **70% of India's plastic recycling** — an unpaid climate mitigation subsidy from the poorest women in the city to everyone else.

Their working surface is the single hottest workplace in the Indian city. Landfill surfaces reach **up to 70°C**. Delhi's ambient peaks hit **49°C**. Every summer, Ghazipur, Bhalswa and Okhla catch fire — decomposing waste generating methane that ignites in the heat. Satellites have detected **124 significant methane leaks** over Delhi since 2020, including a Ghazipur plume releasing **156 tonnes per hour** in 2021. Waste pickers work through the smoke because the price of recovered material does not fall when the temperature rises.

Reported consequences are direct: collapsing from dehydration mid-shift; infections from medical waste inflamed by sun exposure; respiratory damage from fire seasons that now overlap with heat seasons.

**Why this sector is the sharpest test of any heat policy:** these women are simultaneously (a) the most heat-exposed workers in the city, (b) essential municipal infrastructure operating without a contract, and (c) net contributors to climate mitigation. If a heat regime cannot reach them, it is not a heat regime.

### Street vendors
Covered above (§2.3, §3.1). The binding constraints are water, toilets, shade and stock spoilage — infrastructure, not information. India's Street Vendors Act already creates registered vending zones; those zones are the obvious unit for mandated water and shade coverage.

### Home-based workers
The hidden majority. Their exposure is indoor, unmeasured, and invisible to every outdoor-heat instrument India operates. See §2.4.

### Agriculture and MGNREGS
Agriculture bears **66%** of India's heat-related labour-hour losses. Women dominate transplanting, weeding and harvesting — stooped, midday, unshaded tasks. MGNREGS pays equal statutory wages (all-India average ₹285.47/day in 2024-25) but sets **output targets that do not flex with temperature**, which converts heat directly into a wage cut. Worker groups in heat-prone Kalyana Karnataka have formally demanded reduced mandatory hours in peak summer. MGNREGS is, structurally, India's largest ready-made heat-protection instrument — it already reaches heat-exposed rural women at scale and already has a payment rail. It has simply never been configured as one.

### Construction and brick kilns
Female brick moulders lose **5.55–6.48% productivity per 1°C** of WBGT. Night shifts, increasingly used to dodge peak heat, shift the exposure rather than remove it — and for women they add a second unpaid shift at home during the day plus safety and childcare risks at night.

### Salt pan work
The Agariya women of the Little Rann of Kutch: eight months on the salt flats, 45–48°C, no electricity, no healthcare, no permanent shelter, water once every 25 days. The region supplies about a third of India's inland salt.

### Gig and platform work
Growing fast and disproportionately female in platform-linked domestic work, home care and community health roles. In 2025 the NDMA issued an advisory recommending suspension of mandatory work between **11 am and 4 pm** during heat alerts, two-hour shifts with cooling breaks, a **3 km** service-radius cap during peak hours, relaxed delivery deadlines, and **two emergency leave days in summer — three for women workers**. It is an advisory. Nothing in it is enforceable.

### Frontline health workers (ASHA, Anganwadi)
Almost entirely women, paid by incentive per task, doing field visits in peak heat — and simultaneously the people expected to *deliver* heat-health messaging. The obvious fix is also the cheapest: adjust targets during declared heat events while protecting incentive payments, so that the heat-response workforce is not the workforce most penalised by heat.

## 5. Why the policy machinery misses them

### 5.1 Heat Action Plans: gender-blind and occupation-thin
A Centre for Policy Research assessment of **37 Heat Action Plans** (9 city, 13 district, 15 state, across 18 states) found that plans "discuss all women as a homogeneous group, instead of examining how gender intersects with income, caste, livelihoods". Vulnerable-occupation lists reliably name vegetable vendors, mechanics, cab drivers and construction workers — and reliably omit domestic work, home-based work and gig work, which is where women concentrate. Critically: **not one plan that addressed occupation also explicitly acknowledged caste**, despite waste picking and salt-pan work being caste-assigned occupations.

Research on Ahmedabad's heatwave early warning system further shows that *access* to warnings is itself gendered — issuing an alert is not the same as it reaching a woman who is already at the worksite.

### 5.2 Labour law: no heat standard exists
The 2020 consolidation into four labour codes produced **no temperature or ventilation thresholds**, even for factory work; the OSH Code mentions temperature management only for dock workers, and does not recognise heat as an occupational hazard. It also excludes gig workers. The older Factories Act, 1948 at least required "reasonable conditions of comfort" — a weaker instrument in principle, but one that named the obligation.

This is the central legal fact of the whole brief: **in India there is currently no enforceable temperature at which any worker acquires a right to stop.**

### 5.3 Disaster classification and compensation
At least ten states — including Telangana, Rajasthan, Odisha, Andhra Pradesh, Tamil Nadu, Kerala, Karnataka, Maharashtra, Chhattisgarh and Madhya Pradesh — have notified heatwaves as **state-specific disasters**, unlocking State Disaster Response Fund money. But heatwave is **still not a nationally notified disaster** under the Disaster Management Act, 2005, which keeps the national disaster relief pool (~₹1.2 lakh crore) closed to heat — and leaves interstate migrant workers, disproportionately excluded from state-level schemes, with no route at all.

Telangana's 2025 Heatwave Action Plan raised heat-death ex gratia from **₹50,000 to ₹4 lakh** and directed the labour welfare department to ensure rest, drinking water, ORS and shaded rest areas for sanitation and solid waste management workers. That directive is exactly right in substance. Whether it is audited is the open question — and a GHMC sanitation worker in Moula Ali describing heatstroke as a routine part of the job suggests the answer.

### 5.4 Data invisibility
No sex-disaggregated heat mortality for India. No occupation-disaggregated heat morbidity. No systematic measurement of *indoor* workplace temperature, which is where home-based women work. No standard instrument for heat-attributable income loss. Every gap in this brief marked "reported by" rather than "measured at" is a consequence.

### 5.5 Social protection that registers but does not pay
e-Shram has enrolled 31.89 crore workers, 54% of them women, and integrated 13 schemes. Registration is real; benefit delivery is thin; and there is no automatic heat trigger attached to any of it.

## 6. What is already working

These are not proposals. They are running programmes with measured results, which makes them the strongest available argument that the problem is tractable.

**Parametric heat insurance (SEWA with Arsht-Rock / Climate Resilience for All).** Payouts trigger automatically on an objective temperature threshold — for example 40°C on two consecutive days — with no claims process, no assessor, and no proof of loss. Scale: **21,000 women in 2023 → ~50,000 across Gujarat, Maharashtra and Rajasthan in 2024 → ~225,000–250,000 women across 34 districts and seven states in 2025.** The 2024 season paid out **₹2.92 crore (~US$351,000)**, reaching **46,242 women across 16 districts** of Gujarat and Rajasthan, with a maximum of about US$19.80 per member in Dungarpur and an average near US$7.38 elsewhere.

*Honest assessment:* the design is excellent and the amounts are small. An average payout under US$8 is not income replacement; it buys ORS, medicine, a day of groceries. Its real achievement is proving the **rail** works — objective trigger, no claims friction, direct transfer to women, at a quarter-million scale. That rail is what a state wage-compensation scheme should be built on, with a state-sized transfer flowing through it.

**Cool roofs (Mahila Housing Trust).** Solar-reflective paint, ModRoof, thermocol insulation and roof ventilation cut indoor temperatures by **4–7°C** in Ahmedabad slum households, with lower electricity bills. Because women are the ones working, resting and providing care indoors, the benefit is structurally gendered. One documented case: a Bhopal tailor whose daily income rose from **₹150 to ₹450** after solar-reflective paint let her work through the afternoon.

**Waste-picker cooperatives.** SWaCH Pune — **3,500+ member-owners**, mostly women and Dalits — has over 3,000 women servicing **600,000 homes** and recycling **50,000+ tonnes a year**, with negotiated wages, safety equipment and social security. Hasiru Dala in Bengaluru has organised **9,500 waste pickers** and secured government-recognised occupational identity cards. An ID card is the precondition for every other entitlement; a cooperative is the only body that can negotiate heat provisions for workers with no employer.

**Ahmedabad's Heat Action Plan (2013).** South Asia's first, built after the 2010 heatwave's 1,300+ excess deaths. Evaluations associate it with roughly **1,100–1,190 deaths avoided per year**. Early warning plus health-system preparedness plus public messaging demonstrably saves lives — at very low cost. What it does not do is protect income, and it does not close the toilet-and-water gap.

**The NDMA gig advisory (2025).** The right content in the wrong instrument. Its provisions — peak-hour work suspension, cooling breaks, radius caps, extra leave with a specific additional day for women — are a ready-made template for a binding standard.

## 7. An agenda

### Immediate (this budget cycle, no legislation required)

1. **Free water and safe toilets at work, mandated and audited.** 100% coverage of free drinking water and shaded rest points within **200 metres** of every registered vending zone, market, landfill and transfer station — with **free, women-designated, lockable toilets**, and quarterly public reporting of coverage. This single intervention addresses hydration, urogenital disease, dehydration-driven kidney injury and menstrual hygiene at once. It is the highest-return item on this list.
2. **Heat-adjust public employment.** During declared heat alerts, relax MGNREGS output targets without docking wages, shift permissible hours to early morning, and protect ASHA/Anganwadi incentive payments when field visits are unsafe. This uses existing payment rails and requires no new law.
3. **Count properly.** Mandate sex- and occupation-disaggregated recording of heat-related morbidity and mortality, with a standard case definition across NCDC, NCRB and state health departments.
4. **Fund cool roofs for home-based workers.** Treat the roof of a home-based worker as workplace infrastructure and subsidise it accordingly. A 4–7°C indoor reduction is a productivity, health and care-burden intervention simultaneously.

### Structural (requires legislation or notification)

5. **A binding occupational heat standard.** Introduce enforceable WBGT-based thresholds with mandated work–rest ratios, shade and water provision, into the OSH Code and its rules — explicitly covering gig, platform, home-based and self-employed workers, and explicitly using thresholds that account for pregnancy and for women's thermoregulatory baseline. The pregnancy threshold already exists in the literature: **27.5°C WBGT for heavy work, 28.0°C for moderate.**
6. **Notify heatwave as a national disaster** under the Disaster Management Act, 2005, unlocking national relief funds and — critically — covering interstate migrant workers who fall between state schemes.
7. **Build state heat wage-compensation on the parametric rail.** Take SEWA's proven trigger-and-transfer mechanism and put a state-scale transfer through it: a defined day's wage, paid automatically to e-Shram-registered women workers in districts crossing a declared threshold. The plumbing is demonstrated; only the funding is missing.
8. **Rewrite Heat Action Plans with occupation × gender × caste as the vulnerability unit,** not "women" as a category — and co-design them with worker organisations (SEWA, SWaCH, Hasiru Dala, vendor and gig unions) rather than consulting them after drafting. Add **heat audits of informal workplaces employing women** as a standing HAP component.
9. **Treat night heat and indoor heat as policy objects.** Night shelters and cooling centres open overnight; building-material standards and retrofit subsidies for low-income housing; and measurement of indoor temperature where home-based work happens.
10. **Formalise waste work.** Occupational ID cards, cooperative contracts with ULBs, and — since these women are already delivering municipal recycling and avoided emissions — payment for that service, with heat provisions written into the contract.

## 8. Research gaps worth funding

- **Sex-specific heat-attributable mortality for India.** The single largest evidentiary hole. Everything else is advocacy until this exists.
- **Indoor workplace temperature for home-based work.** Nobody is measuring the actual thermal environment where a large share of women's paid work occurs.
- **Longitudinal income effects.** All the good income data is cross-sectional and recall-based. Nobody has followed the same women across multiple heat seasons to measure debt accumulation, asset sales, and labour-force exit.
- **Heat-driven labour force exit, disaggregated by sex.** Female labour force participation is rising while heat exposure is rising. Whether heat is already suppressing that trend is an open, tractable and highly policy-relevant question.
- **Menstrual and menopausal health under occupational heat stress.** Almost entirely unstudied in Indian field settings.
- **Effect sizes for interventions.** Cool roofs have measured temperature effects; nobody has measured their effect on income, health-care spending or care burden with a proper counterfactual.

## 9. Where this connects to waste and sanitation work in Hyderabad

Telangana notified heatwave as a state-specific disaster, published a 2025 Heatwave Action Plan, raised heat-death ex gratia to ₹4 lakh, and directed its labour welfare department to provide rest, water, ORS and shade specifically to sanitation and solid waste management workers. On paper, Hyderabad is ahead of most Indian cities.

The gap is verification. GHMC's waste system runs on roughly 22,000 staff and about 2,000 Swachh Autos, alongside informal pickers at transfer stations and dumpsites, and self-help-group structures that make the workforce heavily female at the collection end. The Telangana MSW worker study found musculoskeletal morbidity in 76.6% of workers and injuries in 39.7% of the Hyderabad sample — before heat is added.

The measurable questions for any citizen-facing waste platform in Hyderabad are therefore concrete: **where are the shaded rest points, water points and functioning women's toilets on the collection routes, at the transfer stations, and at the dumpsite — and how many of them exist during a heat alert?** That is a mappable, auditable, ward-level dataset. A platform that already maps garbage by ward is the natural instrument for mapping the amenities the people who clear that garbage are legally owed.

### A note on evidence quality

Figures in this brief come from three tiers, and they are not equivalent:

- **Peer-reviewed studies** (brick workers, pregnancy cohorts, the toilet-access study, IPV analyses, MSW worker health) — strongest, but usually small and geographically specific.
- **Institutional datasets and reports** (ILO, Lancet Countdown, PLFS, e-Shram, CPR's HAP assessment) — reliable at national scale, thin on gender-by-occupation disaggregation.
- **Organisational surveys reported through media** (SEWA's ~600-member survey, WIEGO's vendor surveys, MSSRF's 3,300-woman study, the per-degree income elasticities) — directionally consistent across independent sources, but exact coefficients should be quoted with attribution rather than as settled facts. Where two summaries of the same study disagreed, this brief reports the more conservative reading.

Full-text verification of several primary sources was not possible from this working environment (outbound access to most publisher domains was blocked), so figures were triangulated across multiple independent secondary reports rather than read off the source PDF. Anything load-bearing for a publication or a submission should be checked against the linked primary document.
